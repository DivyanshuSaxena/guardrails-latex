\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

The Operating System (OS) is responsible for managing a wide variety of resources for a diverse range of applications, all running together, with each application having unique and dynamic demands.
To add further to the complexity of OS tasks, the same OS can be deployed in various scenarios -- from datacenter servers to edge devices to end-user equipment.
As such, various OS policies (and associated interfaces) have been designed and refined over the years to provide good performance for a general set of applications.

However, simply refining these heuristics is not enough for modern applications with tight and strict performance needs. 
Decades of refinement of memory management policies still leaves us with an Operating System that may spend up to 500 ms allocating a huge page~\cite{cbmm}, that may lead to network under-utilization~\cite{} \ds{cite CC papers}, and may not be sub-optimal in scheduling processes \ds{search for scheduling papers from sigmetrics}. 

Learning (in particular, deep learning) has proven to be a useful \ds{better word?} tool in dealing with dynamic environments and out-performing human-designed heuristics in a number of domains such as games, vision, etc.
Operating systems policies haven't remained untouched either -- learning has demonstrated significant benefits when used for OS policies such as I/O latency prediction~\cite{linnOS-osdi-20}, file readahead~\cite{kml-hotstorage-21}, congestion control~\cite{orca-sigcomm-20}, among others.
This has been largely attributed to the ability of deep learning models to use a wide variety of features, infer future behaviors accurately and generalize across examples.

However, there is a reluctance of OS practitioners in using these learned models, majority of which stems from the uncertainty in the decision quality of learned policies.
Policies employing deep neural networks suffer from a lack of worst-case analysis due to the black-box nature of the neural networks. \ds{Elaborate more.}
We believe this is also a major roadblock in ushering novel learned policies for the OS at the same pace as has been observed in other domains, such as protein discovery, biomedicine, etc. \discuss{What else to say to motivate guardrails?}

Hesitation reasons:
1. Black box nature of guardrails leading to us not trusting the model, because of: i) security, ii) reproducibility. \textit{Guardrails help in regulating and understanding these black box models.}

2. Misbehaviors where the learned policies may take unsafe decisions leading to drastically bad system states. regression because of updates.

3. Performance overheads of using models. \textit{Bounding performance impact by monitoring overheads and impact}

4. Training Data and Mechanism.

In this paper, we attempt to develop a framework to provide guarantees on how bad a learned OS policy can be and provide means to detect and regulate when the policy leads to arbitrarily bad system behavior.
We call this framework \textbf{Guardrails for the OS}.
We describe the key requirements and design principles for building these guardrails, and then devise a taxonomy for building standardized guardrails.

\ds{Refine:}
Our framework utilizes a simple interface for specifying desired high-level properties that OS practitioners want their policies to satisfy.
This interface uses a declarative query language to allow reasoning over events and collected data points.
