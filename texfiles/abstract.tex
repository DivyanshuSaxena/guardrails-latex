\begin{abstract}
OS policies are increasingly specialized to particular workloads and hardware because generic heuristics often fall short. But such policies typically rely on assumptions about workload and platform behavior, and when those assumptions fail in deployment, performance can silently degrade. Existing practice relies on extensive offline analysis to diagnose these regressions, which is poorly suited to deployment-time failures and offers little guidance for future policy refinement. We argue that OSes need \emph{performance introspection}: the ability to check during execution whether a policy is meeting its performance requirements and to intervene when it is not. To this end, we propose \emph{OS guardrails}, which pair a runtime-checkable performance condition with a corrective action. We develop principles for designing guardrails, a Guardrail Description Language (\lang) and semantics for specifying them over kernel-visible signals, an implementation substrate using eBPF-based monitors, and a verification interface for relating local guardrail conditions and repairs to system-wide performance objectives. Across six case studies, guardrails reveal broken deployment-time assumptions, identify policy-specific failure modes, and enable targeted corrections or safer fallbacks.
\end{abstract}
